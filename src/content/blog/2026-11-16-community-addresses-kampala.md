In December 2025 I stayed with my missionary friends Ben and Dorothy in Kampala. Within a day I noticed something I had never had to think about: most places there have no address.

## "Call me when you reach the kiosk"

Ask someone in Kampala where they live and you get directions, not a number: past the MTN kiosk, the blue gate, then ask again. That works among neighbours. It works much less well for anyone who doesn't already know you.

Getting a boda boda (a motorcycle taxi) or a car from a ride-hailing app usually meant a phone call: the driver calls, you describe the turn, the driver calls again, and in the end you walk out to the main road and wave. Even Bolt, the Estonian app I praise elsewhere on this site, can only take you to a pin on a map; the last hundred metres run on phone calls. It's tolerable for a ride. It is a different matter for a delivery, a nurse or an ambulance.

## What I built

So in the evenings at Ben and Dorothy's I started building [Community Addresses](https://communityaddresses.org), now a project of Open Mission of Hope. It gives an address to every building that doesn't have one.

- **It builds on OpenStreetMap.** Volunteers have already mapped a great many buildings in Uganda. Community Addresses gives each of them an address and leaves OpenStreetMap as the source of truth for the map itself.
- **The addresses read like addresses.** `30 C-105, KAM, Uganda` means house 30 on street 105 in the central part of the Kampala region. Streets that already have names keep them: `175 Yusuf Lule Road, KAM, Uganda`.
- **There is room to grow.** Houses are numbered in tens and unnamed streets in fives, so a new building or road can slot in between without renumbering its neighbours.
- **The regions follow geography, not politics.** They are drawn around cities, lakes and mountains rather than district lines that get redrawn, so an address can last decades.
- **It never pretends to be official.** Community addresses show in orange and official ones in green, and every community address is explicitly temporary and reversible.
- **The local knowledge stays.** Each building can carry access notes such as "blue gate after the MTN kiosk", the directions people already give, which neighbours can confirm or correct.
- **It works on the phones people have.** It runs in the browser, keeps working offline once loaded, and needs only a phone number to contribute.

## Why it matters to me

An address is a small thing, but it changes who can find you. Without one, you are findable only through people who already know you. With one, a stranger can reach you: a driver, a courier, a doctor. Addresses turn "who you know" into "where you are", and that is a quiet kind of freedom.

It is not a replacement for an official addressing system, and the hard part isn't the software but getting people to use it. If you live in Kampala or work there, try it: look up your building, and add the note you'd give a boda driver.
