When I spent two weeks in Finland, from Helsinki and Vantaa up to Haapavesi, Oulu and Tornio, the words that kept coming to mind were *clean* and *quiet*. Part of what sits behind that calm is a policy that sounds almost too simple: if someone has no home, give them one first.

## A lease, not a bed

Most countries treat housing as a reward. Get sober, get treatment, prove you're "ready", and maybe you'll move up from a shelter bed to a room. Finland flipped the order. Under Housing First, a person gets an ordinary apartment with their own rental contract and their own key, and support comes after, if they want it. Nobody has to earn a door that locks.

That is what I love about it. It treats a homeless person as an adult: someone who can sign a lease, close the door and decide for themselves. A shelter tells you when to come and go. A home lets you choose.

## How Finland built it

It wasn't a cultural accident. It was an institution, built on purpose.

- In 2008 the government launched PAAVO, a national program aimed squarely at long-term homelessness, followed by PAAVO II (2012–2015) and AUNE (2016–2019), which focused on prevention.
- The Y-Foundation (Y-Säätiö), founded in the 1980s, became the landlord, buying and building apartments across the country.
- Helsinki turned its shelters into homes. In 2008 the city had nearly 600 shelter and hostel beds; by 2016 only 52 emergency beds were left. The Salvation Army's 250-bed hostel became 81 independent apartments with staff on site.

The result: long-term homelessness in Finland fell by about 70% between 2008 and 2023, according to the state housing agency ARA (since 2026, Varke), to about 1,000 people in a country of 5.6 million.

## Then it slipped

Here is the part the "Finland solved homelessness" headlines leave out. In 2024 the national count rose to 3,806, up 377. For 2025 the Y-Foundation reports about 4,579, the largest rise on record, and in Helsinki the number of people experiencing homelessness on their own rose to 979.

The Y-Foundation points to cuts to the housing allowance, a halved budget for housing advice and rising rents. That is their analysis, and the causes can be argued over. But the shape of the story is clear: the same country and the same model, and the trend turned within two years of the support around it being cut.

## The lesson

I believe good outcomes come from institutions, not from ancestry, and Finland is my favorite evidence: a country that took the Nordic model and made it its own. Housing First shows the other half of that belief. Institutions aren't trophies you win once. They are kept up, or they decay. A home first only works while someone keeps paying for the homes, the advice and the support.

## What I'm doing about it

That's why, through Open Mission of Hope, I'm building [OMH Heartbeat](https://heartbeat.openmissionofhope.org): a small tool for a gap that housing alone doesn't close. If someone who asked to be noticed stops being seen, a named, accountable person follows up. The first pilot we're proposing is with people experiencing homelessness around Asheville, North Carolina. It isn't a home, but it says what Finland says: you matter, and someone will notice.

A key first. Then help. Then keep the lights on.
